The cheapest NAS we'll quote costs ₹62,000: a 2-bay unit with two 2 TB drives. We're sometimes asked why we don't offer a single drive, which would bring the price down to around ₹43,000.

The short answer is that a single-drive NAS gives up the one thing a NAS is for.

## A NAS is shared, and that raises the stakes

A drive in one person's laptop holds one person's work. A NAS holds everyone's: the shared folders, the project files, the accounts, often the backups of everyone's laptops too. When that single drive fails, the whole office loses its data at once.

And drives do fail. Every drive eventually wears out, and some fail early. The question isn't whether a drive in your NAS will fail, but whether you'll lose anything when it does.

## Two drives change the answer

With two drives mirrored (RAID 1), every file is written to both. When one fails, the NAS carries on from the other. You replace the failed drive, the NAS copies the data back onto it, and nobody loses a file.

That's why RAID starts at two drives. A single drive isn't an array at all — it has nothing to fall back on. RAID 0 also needs two drives, but it's built for speed and survives no failures, so for a first NAS we recommend a mirror.

## The difference is ₹19,000

On our price list, the step from one drive to two is a single extra 2 TB drive: ₹19,000. For that, a drive failure goes from losing everything to a replacement drive and an afternoon's rebuild.

As the NAS grows, the cost of protection falls. With four drives in RAID 5, one drive's worth of space protects the other three. With eight, one drive protects seven.

## Protection isn't backup

A mirror protects you from a failed drive. It doesn't protect you from a deleted folder, a ransomware attack, a power surge that takes out the whole unit, or a fire. For that you need a backup: a separate copy, ideally kept somewhere else.

So a two-drive NAS isn't where data protection ends. It's where it starts.
